# AI Tells

Patterns to avoid in all written content. Read the examples in `references/` for voice - don't follow rules mechanically.

## The One Rule

**Never use the correlative construction.** This is the #1 AI tell:
- "X isn't just Y - it's Z"
- "It's not about X, it's about Y"
- "Schools aren't just buildings - they're communities"

Find another way. State the thing directly.

## Other Patterns to Avoid

1. **Setup phrases:** "The best part? ..." / "The secret? ..." / "Here's the thing..."
2. **Staccato fragments:** "No fluff. No filler. Just results."
3. **Triple adjectives:** "Fast, efficient, reliable" (grouping in threes)
4. **Cliché openers:** "In today's fast-paced..." / "In the ever-evolving..."
5. **Dramatic punctuation:** "Let that sink in" / "Now more than ever"
6. **Thesaurus abuse:** utilize, implement, facilitate, leverage, comprehensive, crucial
7. **Fake questions:** "Sound familiar?" / "Ready to level up?"
8. **Transition tells:** "And here's the kicker" / "Enter: [thing]"

## Format Constraints

- **Dashes:** hyphens with spaces - like this - not em dashes (—)
- **No emojis** in body content
- **No bold** for emphasis within paragraphs

# Justin Mikolay Writing Style

*Discovery by Deletion - Maximum Impact with Minimum Words*

## Core Philosophy

Justin Mikolay's approach centers on "discovery by deletion"—figuring out what you want to say by deleting what you don't.

> "To write something, you have to move from left to right (draft new sentences; create fresh text). But to write something well, you have to move from right to left (locate your favorite sentences and delete them)."

**Essence:** Make ideas BIGGER while making word count SMALLER. Every sentence must earn its place.

---

## The Five Games of Writing

Mikolay describes five "games" that writers play, with each game representing a deeper level of mastery:

### 1. The Novelty Game
**Beginners focus on originality**
- "Can I come up with something new?"
- Often leads to overwrought, show-offy writing
- Prioritizes cleverness over clarity

### 2. The Ideas Game
**Intermediate writers focus on clear concepts**
- "Do I have a clear idea to express?"
- Learning to organize thoughts logically
- Finding the core insight

### 3. The Emotions Game
**Advanced writers connect ideas to feelings**
- "Does this hit people's hearts, not just heads?"
- Understanding emotional resonance
- Writing that moves readers

### 4. The Movement Game
**Expert writers create flow and sequence**
- "Does this move smoothly from thought to thought?"
- Mastering pacing and structure
- Creating inevitable progression

### 5. The Stillness Game
**Masters understand the power of what's left unsaid**
- "What's the power of absence and white space?"
- Understanding what NOT to say
- Letting readers' imagination fill gaps

**Key insight:** Great writers play all five games at once on a good day. The hard part is playing them consistently.

---

## Key Principles

### 1. Focus on Ideas, Not Words

> "I wrote for a decade before I internalized this truth. My work suffered because I tried to write something memorable or original instead of just writing what I meant."

**How this works:**
- Don't search for a phrase at the expense of an idea
- Don't write something because you like how it sounds
- Don't make a grab for insight or try to be memorable
- Just write what you mean

**The shift:** From "How do I make this sound good?" to "What am I actually trying to say?"

### 2. Make Ideas Bigger While Making Word Count Smaller

> "If you want to write something compelling, you have to add an axis to your creative process. You have to make the ideas bigger and the word count smaller at the same time."

**How leaders edit (and you should too):**
- Every time they edit, they move up and to the left
- At the same time they're making the draft shorter, they're making the ideas bigger
- This is the opposite of most people (who make drafts longer when trying to add impact)

**Example:**
- Before: "Our innovative curriculum approach incorporates multiple methodologies"
- After: "We teach the way your child learns"

### 3. Be Simple AND Universal

> "If you want to design something attractive, you have to make the ideas simple and universal at the same time."

**Simple isn't enough.** The most compelling writing:
- Makes complex ideas accessible
- Appeals to universal human experiences
- Can be understood by virtually anyone
- Resonates across different contexts

**Not just simple = jargon reduction**
**Simple AND universal = deep truth expressed plainly**

### 4. Create with Emotions, Not Just Logic

> "The song '22' isn't designed to appeal to 22-year-olds, it's designed to appeal to the 22-year-old in each of us. It overlaps ideas and emotions like hands on a clock."

**The principle:**
- Connect to readers emotionally, not just intellectually
- Speak to universal human experiences
- Hit both the head AND the heart at the same time

**Examples of mastery:**
- Carl Sagan's writing
- Fred Rogers' communication
- J.K. Rowling's storytelling
- All combine intellectual substance with emotional resonance

### 5. Structure Creates Movement

> "Writing brings ideas and emotions in line, and it moves: it takes the reader from one thought to the next, in sequence and at the right pace."

**Effective structure:**
- Creates natural flow from one idea to the next
- Maintains perfect pace (neither rushed nor dragging)
- Never achieves precision in one area at the expense of another
- Takes readers on a journey

**Key insight:** Structure isn't decoration; it's how you move readers through ideas.

### 6. Understand the Power of What's Not Said

> "A great performance isn't about showing things, but hiding them."

**Master writers understand:**
- The power of white space and silence
- What to omit is as important as what to include
- The reader's imagination fills gaps more powerfully than explicit statements
- Comic books teach this: "All the action happens in between the panels"

---

## The Writing Process: Three Integrated Components

> "Reading, thinking, and writing aren't separate acts. They're three parts of one act (the act of creation)."

### 1. Reading
"Filling myself with ideas" — knowing what others have learned

### 2. Thinking
"Clarifying and ordering ideas" — expressing them in deliberate sequence

### 3. Writing
"Reflecting ideas back to myself in written form"

**Critical insight:** "Let any one of these processes be in haste and it will stifle the others. Keep each in rhythm and they'll fuel each other."

**Application:**
- Don't rush through reading
- Don't skip thinking/organizing
- Don't treat writing as transcription
- Keep all three in balance

---

## Practical Writing Advice

### Writing IS Thinking (Literally)

> "Your sentences ARE your thinking. When you're writing and in flow, you're not 'coming up with' ideas, you're playing with ideas that are already there."

**The key insight:** The writing happens on the page, not in your head.

**How this works:**
- Don't try to perfect ideas mentally before writing
- Get ideas out of your head and onto the page
- Play with the words and ideas you see in front of you
- Discover what you think through the act of writing

### The Three-Stage Creative Cycle

**Stage 1: Create** — Make new parts
**Stage 2: Delete** — Remove favorite parts
**Stage 3: Share** — Publish and get feedback

> "The strategy that works in one stage of the creative process causes failure in the next."

**Key principle:** Don't edit while creating. Don't create while editing. These are different modes.

### Writing Rules to Live By

1. "Let the meaning choose the word, and not the other way around" (George Orwell)
2. Convey your intended ideas, and none more
3. Speak only when you have something to say
4. Speak such that you cannot be misunderstood

**Application:** These are filters, not rules. Use them to cut away what doesn't serve.

---

## The Deletion Practice

### Step 1: Write Your First Draft
- Don't hold back
- Get all your ideas down
- Don't edit yet

### Step 2: Identify Your Favorite Sentences
- What do you love?
- What are you proud of?
- Write these down

### Step 3: DELETE THEM
- Remove what you love most
- This breaks attachment to clever phrases
- Forces you to replace with better ideas

### Step 4: Replace with Something Clearer
- In fewer words
- More direct
- More powerful

### Step 5: Repeat
- Keep deleting and replacing
- Until you can't delete anymore
- Until every sentence earns its place

**Example:**
- **First draft:** "The multifaceted nature of contemporary educational paradigms, which encompasses diverse learning modalities and pedagogical approaches, necessitates a comprehensive reevaluation of traditional assessment methodologies."
- **After deletion:** "Schools teach in many ways now. We need to rethink how we test."

---

## How to Apply This Style

### 1. Write What You Mean
- Not what sounds clever
- Not what sounds impressive
- What you actually think

### 2. Express Bigger Ideas in Fewer Words
- Every word must earn its place
- Cut favorites when they're not essential
- Let ideas breathe in white space

### 3. Make Ideas Both Simple and Universal
- Accessible to anyone
- True to universal human experience
- Profound in its simplicity

### 4. Connect Emotionally AND Intellectually
- Hit both head and heart
- Appeal to universal experience
- Create resonance, not just information

### 5. Create Natural Flow
- Structure guides readers smoothly
- Pacing is intentional
- One thought leads naturally to the next

### 6. Embrace Silence
- What you leave unsaid matters
- Let readers' imagination fill gaps
- White space is powerful

---

## Voice Consistency Checklist

### Before Publishing, Ask:

- [ ] **Is this what I actually mean?** (Not just what sounds good)
- [ ] **Could any words be removed?** (Does every word earn its place?)
- [ ] **Is the idea expressed simply?** (Could a 12-year-old understand it?)
- [ ] **Is it universal?** (Does it resonate beyond specific context?)
- [ ] **Does it hit head AND heart?** (Intellectual + emotional?)
- [ ] **Does it flow naturally?** (Do ideas move smoothly?)
- [ ] **Is anything left unsaid?** (Does imagination fill gaps?)
- [ ] **Have I deleted my favorite parts?** (Am I over-attached?)

---

## Key Takeaway

The Justin Mikolay style works through **disciplined deletion**, **universal truths expressed plainly**, and **perfect pacing**. It requires:

- **Courage to delete** your favorite phrases
- **Clarity about your core idea** (idea-first, not word-first)
- **Understanding of emotional resonance** (not just logical flow)
- **Mastery of structure** (how ideas move readers)
- **Respect for silence** (what you don't say matters)

Master Mikolay's approach when you need maximum impact with limited words, when you're distilling complex ideas into memorable statements, or when you're writing for both the mind and the heart.

# One Thing Better Writing Style

*Decision Frameworks and Risk Reframing*

## Core Philosophy

The One Thing Better voice uses framework-driven narratives to help readers make better decisions. It teaches readers HOW TO THINK about decisions and tradeoffs, not just WHAT to decide.

**Essence:** Every decision has risks on both sides—risks of action AND risks of inaction. Once you see that clearly, you become empowered to choose.

**Core principle:** You're not just informing readers; you're teaching them a repeatable thinking pattern they can apply to their own decisions.

---

## Fundamental Insight: Every Decision Has Two Costs

### The Problem with How We Think About Decisions

When we decide whether to do something, we ask ourselves two questions:
1. What's the upside to doing this?
2. What's the downside to doing this?

**But there's something missing:** What happens if you don't do that thing?

### The Solution: Risk of Action + Risk of Inaction

There are risks of action AND risks of inaction. Every decision has a cost—either because of what we do, or what we do not do.

**You cannot truly know the wisdom of a decision—and therefore decide whether to make that decision—until you explore the costs of not making the decision.**

### The Framework

When facing a decision, always ask:

**If I do nothing:** What happens? What are the actual consequences?

**If I do something:** What happens? What are the real consequences?

Then compare: Which scenario has the better potential outcome? Which risks are you better equipped to handle?

---

## How This Works: The Real Example

### The Scenario: Steve's Dilemma

Steve is a former prison inmate trying to turn his life around. He landed a full-time job offer after freelance work with a major company. His supervisor said: "Just fill these forms out. We'll do a background check, and then let's get to work."

Steve panicked. A background check would reveal his conviction. Should he tell his supervisor about it first?

### Analyzing the Risks

**If Steve does nothing:**
- The company will run a background check
- They'll discover his conviction
- Company executives discuss it privately
- Steve doesn't know what they're thinking
- They might wonder why he didn't tell them
- They might assume he tried to hide it

**If Steve does something:**
- Steve tells his manager before the background check
- He explains his crime, punishment, and efforts to move forward
- His manager hears his story directly
- Steve owns his narrative
- He shows he has nothing to hide

### The Key Insight

In both scenarios, the company will find out about his conviction. The only difference is HOW they find out and what story gets told.

**The risk of inaction = Company discovers it secretly + wonders about hidden motives**
**The risk of action = Company learns from Steve directly + Steve controls the narrative**

### The Decision

After going through this exercise, Steve decided to be upfront. His supervisor thanked him, said he was brave, and promised he'd still get the job.

Because Steve took action, he now has the opportunity he wanted and can restart his life.

---

## Key Stylistic Elements

### 1. Personal Story as Framework Illustration

**What it is:** Using a real person's story to make an abstract framework concrete.

**How to use:**
- Introduce a character and their dilemma
- Show the false framework people usually use
- Introduce the true framework (costs of action AND inaction)
- Apply the framework to the character's story
- Generalize back to reader

**Why it works:** Stories stick. Frameworks are abstract. Together, they're powerful.

### 2. Risk Analysis (Both Sides)

**What it is:** Systematically examining what could go wrong if you act, and what could go wrong if you don't.

**How to use:**
- State both scenarios clearly
- What's the risk of action?
- What's the risk of inaction?
- Which risks are acceptable?
- Which can you live with?

**Never:** Frame one side as obviously better without showing both costs

### 3. The Reframing Move

**What it is:** Showing how the same situation looks different when you change your perspective.

**Example:**
- **Old frame:** "Either I get this job or I don't"
- **New frame:** "Either I tell my truth and control the story, or I hide and hope they don't find out"

**How it works:** Changes what the decision is really about

### 4. Teaching a Thinking Pattern

**What it is:** The reader should walk away with a framework they can apply to other decisions.

**How to achieve:**
- Explicitly name the framework ("Every decision has costs on both sides")
- Show it applied to the example
- Show how readers could apply it themselves
- Give them the questions to ask

**Application examples:**
- Making a career change
- Having a difficult conversation
- Speaking up about something
- Making an unpopular choice

---

## Decision-Making Framework

### Step 1: Name the Decision
What are you actually deciding between?
- Not "Should I do X?" but "Do I tell the truth or hide it?"
- Not "Should I change jobs?" but "Do I stay comfortable or risk growth?"

### Step 2: Identify the False Framework
What's the common way people think about this decision?
- Usually oversimplified
- Usually ignores costs of inaction
- Usually creates false binary

### Step 3: Introduce the True Framework
- Costs of action (real, specific)
- Costs of inaction (real, specific)
- Neither side is safe
- Question: Which risks can you actually live with?

### Step 4: Apply to the Specific Situation
- Use concrete details
- Show the character thinking through both sides
- Show them choosing based on the framework
- Show the results

### Step 5: Generalize Back to Reader
- How could readers use this framework?
- What other decisions could it apply to?
- What questions should they ask themselves?

---

## The Reframing Principle

### Common False Frames
- "Action is risky, inaction is safe" ← FALSE
- "I should avoid all risk" ← IMPOSSIBLE
- "The safest choice is to do nothing" ← OFTEN BACKWARDS
- "Some options have no risks" ← FANTASY

### The Truth
- **Both action and inaction have risks**
- **You cannot avoid risk, only choose which risks**
- **Doing nothing is also a decision with consequences**
- **Empowerment comes from seeing both sides clearly**

---

## How to Apply This Style

### 1. Start with a Real Dilemma
- Use a person facing a genuine decision
- Make their stakes clear
- Show their fear or hesitation

### 2. Show the False Framework
- How are they currently thinking about it?
- What assumption are they making?
- Why is it incomplete?

### 3. Introduce the True Framework
- Both action and inaction have costs
- Which costs can they actually live with?
- Which scenario has the better potential outcome?

### 4. Apply Systematically
- "If you do nothing: X, Y, Z"
- "If you do something: A, B, C"
- Show the actual consequences

### 5. Make the Decision Clear
- Not what they should do (that's their choice)
- But what becomes possible once they see both sides
- How empowerment comes from clarity

### 6. Generalize the Pattern
- What's the framework?
- How could readers apply it?
- What decisions is this useful for?

---

## Voice Consistency Checklist

### Before Publishing, Ask:

- [ ] **Is there a real person with a real dilemma?**
- [ ] **Do I show the false framework people usually use?**
- [ ] **Do I clearly lay out risks of BOTH action and inaction?**
- [ ] **Is the reframe genuine, not manipulative?**
- [ ] **Can readers apply this framework to their own decisions?**
- [ ] **Do I respect that they might choose differently?**
- [ ] **Is the story specific enough to feel real?**
- [ ] **Does the pattern generalize beyond the specific case?**

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### 1. Being Manipulative
**Bad:** Using the framework to push a specific decision
**Good:** Using the framework to clarify thinking, respecting their choice

### 2. Oversimplifying
**Bad:** Pretending hard decisions are easy once you "see" the framework
**Good:** Acknowledging that both sides have real costs

### 3. Using Generic Examples
**Bad:** Abstract dilemmas that don't feel real
**Good:** Specific, detailed stories that ground the framework

### 4. Missing the Generalization
**Bad:** Teaching a lesson that only applies to one situation
**Good:** Teaching a thinking pattern that's broadly applicable

### 5. False Binaries
**Bad:** "Either you do this or that happens"
**Good:** "Here are the real tradeoffs"

---

## Real-World Applications

### Career Decisions
- **Old frame:** "Should I change jobs?"
- **New frame:** "What are the risks of staying? What are the risks of leaving?"

### Difficult Conversations
- **Old frame:** "Should I tell them?"
- **New frame:** "What's the risk if I speak up? What's the risk if I stay silent?"

### Parenting Decisions
- **Old frame:** "What's the best choice for my child?"
- **New frame:** "What are the costs of this approach? What are the costs of the alternative?"

### Educational Choices
- **Old frame:** "Should we try a different school?"
- **New frame:** "What risks do we take by staying? What risks by changing?"

---

## Key Takeaway

The One Thing Better style works because it:

1. **Teaches thinking, not answers** - Readers walk away with a framework, not just a story
2. **Honors complexity** - Acknowledges both action and inaction have costs
3. **Empowers readers** - Shows how clarity about tradeoffs leads to better decisions
4. **Uses stories effectively** - Makes frameworks concrete and memorable
5. **Is broadly applicable** - The same thinking pattern works across different contexts

Use this style when your goal is to help readers think better about decisions, not just understand a specific situation. It's most powerful for readers facing genuine dilemmas where clarity about tradeoffs is more valuable than a prescribed answer.

# Packy McCormick Writing Style

*Analytical Enthusiasm with Accessible Complexity*

## Core Writing Philosophy

The Packy McCormick voice mixes deep analysis with genuine enthusiasm, using accessible language for complex topics. It's analytical but excited, substantive but personality-driven.

**Essence:** You're a smart person who genuinely loves what you're analyzing, and you want to share that enthusiasm while helping readers understand complex ideas.

**Core Philosophy:** Analysis doesn't have to be boring. Smart analysis can be enthusiastic. You can be rigorous AND excited, substantive AND accessible.

---

## Voice & Tone

### Conversational Optimism

**What it is:** Genuine excitement about developments, paired with accessible explanation.

**Examples:**
- "Hi friends 👋, Happy Friday and welcome back to our 138th Weekly Dose of Optimism."
- "We hope that your week was tarrific and not too tarrifying." (after Trump passed tariffs)
- "It's unclear where the value will ultimately accrue amongst these companies. But what is certainly clear is that consumers are going to get cheaper, faster, better AI products thanks to this period of AI company knife-fighting."
- "We like telling cancer to go fuck itself in the Dose, but it feels good to say it to heart disease, an even bigger American killer, too. So get fucked, heart disease."

**The Pattern:** Signal excitement and optimism about positive developments, while being honest about challenges.

**Application:** When discussing trends or developments, show genuine enthusiasm without losing analytical rigor.

### Accessible Analysis

**What it is:** Explaining complex topics in language anyone can understand.

**Examples:**
- "AI is making money moves into pharma. Alphabet's drug discovery startup, Isomorphic Labs, just raised $600M in its first-ever external funding round—led by Thrive Capital, a major backer of OpenAI. The smart money sees AI not just as a chatbot engine, but as a future drugmaker."
- "You may have never heard of lipoprotein(a), but it can kill you. It's linked to a 2x higher risk of heart attacks and strokes for 1 in 5 people."

**The Pattern:** Start with stakes or relevance, then explain the technical details in accessible terms.

**Application:** Make complex topics feel engaging and relevant before diving into explanation.

### Personal Perspective

**What it is:** Establishing author voice and point of view.

**Examples:**
- "I have a hot take. Or at least, in the context of our current economic policies, it's a hot take. Competition is good for consumers."
- "My perspective is that it's ultimately even better for the consumer."
- "We can now stream thoughts to voice."
- "Packy here. I've been having a ton of fun interviewing the people behind my favorite essays..."

**The Pattern:** Establish yourself as a character in the story, not just a reporter.

**Application:** When presenting analysis, signal your personal take and why you care.

---

## Sentence Techniques

### Dramatic Variation in Length

**What it is:** Mix very short, punchy sentences with longer explanatory ones.

**Examples:**
- "We can now stream thoughts to voice." (5 words)
- "It's competition on steroids, and for the most part, it's all happening right here in the US." (17 words)
- Short sentences in succession for emphasis:
  - "When OpenAI raises $40B, that's good for consumers."
  - "It's also good for OpenAI, to be clear."
  - "But my perspective is that it's ultimately even better for the consumer."
  - "It increases competition."

**How to use:**
- Create rhythm with sentence length variation
- Use very short sentences to emphasize key points
- Longer sentences for explanation and context
- Repeat short sentences in succession for builds

**Application:** Plan sentence length deliberately, not randomly. Short sentences = emphasis.

### Strategic Fragments

**What it is:** Using incomplete sentences for impact.

**Examples:**
- "No need to vocalize, no keyboards, just pure thoughts → language."
- "Generated image"
- "Image"

**How to use:**
- Use fragments after longer sentences for contrast
- Fragments should still make sense grammatically (just be missing subject/verb)
- Never overuse (limit to 1-2 per 500 words)

### One-Line Punches

**What it is:** Standalone statements that land with impact.

**Examples:**
- "That's according to new, clever research coming out of the UK."
- "The shingles vaccine may delay dementia."
- "So get fucked, heart disease."

**How to use:**
- Place after explanation as a summary
- Can be surprising or confirmatory
- Should feel like an insight, not just a fact

---

## Distinctive Style Elements

### Wordplay & Casual Language

**What it is:** Using contemporary language and occasional wordplay to maintain personality.

**Examples:**
- "We hope that your week was tarrific and not too tarrifying."
- "Its smaller competitors, like Anthropic or more verticalized AI players, are probably like 'ohh shit, no way we can't compete on capital with them...'"
- "AI company knife-fighting"

**How to use:**
- Use sparingly, not as a crutch
- Should feel natural, not forced
- Contemporary language adds personality

### Bridging Complex Ideas with Accessible Explanations

**What it is:** Explaining technical complexity without oversimplifying.

**Examples:**
- "High levels can clog arteries and dramatically increase the risk of heart attacks and strokes, especially in people with no other clear risk factors and, frustratingly, it's unaffected by diet, exercise, or most existing drugs."
- "The researchers took advantage of a strict age cutoff — those born just one week apart had vastly different vaccine rates but were otherwise identical, allowing for unusually strong causal evidence."

**How to use:**
- Explain what it means before diving into technical details
- Use concrete examples and comparisons
- Show why it matters, not just what it is

### Blending Analysis with Enthusiasm

**What it is:** Combining intellectual rigor with genuine excitement.

**Examples:**
- "How this technology will evolve is anyone's guess: wearables, use cases outside of the disabled community, no one ever actually speaking again…who knows? But this is the type of SciFi-y, Telepathy Tapes-esque breakthrough we absolutely love to cover in the Weekly Dose."
- "If outcomes match biomarkers, this could be the statin moment for genetically-driven heart disease."

**How to use:**
- Acknowledge uncertainty while maintaining enthusiasm
- Show why the development matters
- Connect to bigger implications

### Connecting Dots Between Developments

**What it is:** Showing how individual news stories fit into larger trends.

**Examples:**
- "AI is moving upstream into biology, and the companies with the deepest models and pockets may soon shape the future of medicine."
- "The study's findings support the theory that either herpesviruses themselves contribute to dementia or that the immune activation from live vaccines like Zostavax provides off-target neuroprotection."

**How to use:**
- After explaining a development, zoom out to show implications
- Connect to macro trends when relevant
- Show how multiple pieces of news fit together

---

## How to Apply This Style

### 1. Mix Deep Analysis with Genuine Enthusiasm
- Don't be afraid to show excitement about developments
- Provide substantive insights while maintaining personality

### 2. Vary Sentence Length Dramatically
- Use very short sentences after longer explanations
- Create rhythm through intentional length variation

### 3. Write Like You're Explaining to a Smart Friend
- Maintain intellectual substance while using casual language
- Assume reader intelligence, not technical knowledge

### 4. Add Personality Through Unexpected Phrases
- Occasional wordplay or contemporary language
- Direct opinions while being accurate
- Conversational asides that show author personality

### 5. Connect Individual Developments to Bigger Patterns
- Help readers see how specific news fits into larger trends
- Show implications and connections between stories
- Build understanding of broader movements

---

## Voice Consistency Checklist

### Before Publishing, Ask:

- [ ] **Does this show genuine enthusiasm?** (For positive developments)
- [ ] **Is the analysis accessible without being oversimplified?**
- [ ] **Do sentence lengths vary intentionally?**
- [ ] **Is my personal perspective clear?**
- [ ] **Are technical terms explained in accessible language?**
- [ ] **Do I show why this matters to readers?**
- [ ] **Are connections to bigger trends clear?**
- [ ] **Does this sound like conversation with a smart friend?**
- [ ] **Is there personality alongside substance?**

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### 1. Sacrificing Substance for Personality
**Bad:** Making everything cute or clever at expense of analysis
**Good:** Substantive analysis with personality that doesn't distract

### 2. Being Too Casual
**Bad:** Using slang that doesn't fit your audience
**Good:** Contemporary language that feels natural

### 3. Losing the Enthusiasm
**Bad:** Sounding detached or cynical
**Good:** Showing genuine excitement about positive developments

### 4. Over-complicating Simple Ideas
**Bad:** Using jargon when simple language works
**Good:** Complex ideas explained accessibly

### 5. Missing the Bigger Picture
**Bad:** Reporting facts without connecting to implications
**Good:** Showing how developments fit into broader trends

---

## Key Takeaway

The Packy McCormick voice works because it combines **intellectual rigor** with **genuine enthusiasm**. Readers trust that you understand the complexity while also believing you're excited about positive developments. You're not just reporting facts—you're helping them understand what matters and why.

The style requires:
- Deep analysis (do the work)
- Accessible language (respect reader intelligence)
- Genuine enthusiasm (show you care)
- Personality (be a character, not a robot)
- Bigger-picture thinking (show connections and implications)

# Pirate Wires Style - Real Examples from Published Newsletters

*11 Real Examples of Pirate Wires Voice in Action*

---

## Example 1: The AI Study Analysis

**Source:** "Kid Rot" segment and follow-up analysis

### The Text:

"A new MIT study claims LLM users 'consistently underperform' on neural and linguistic tests — and AI doomers are obsessed. A professor on Bluesky declares the 'death knell' of AI, and normies are 'terrified' ChatGPT will rot our brains. # Pirate Wires Writing Style

*Authentic, Conversational, Contrarian Voice*

## Core Writing Philosophy

The Pirate Wires voice is authentic, conversational writing for smart people who don't take themselves too seriously but have something meaningful to say. It's the opposite of corporate speak, academic jargon, or media buzzwords.

**Essence:** Write like you're having a conversation with a smart friend. Real, unpolished (in a good way), unapologetically human.

---

## Key Observations About This Style

### 1. Strong Point of View

**What it is:** Unafraid to take contrarian positions that, when you think about it, are actually obvious truths.

**Examples:**
- "You don't have to pay for your kids to play outside. You can just force them to, for free."
- "18 'elite' college students cheating their way through a 20-minute psych experiment with taxpayer funding isn't 'terrifying,' it's pathetic."
- "Congratulations, D.C., it only took you 50 years of local authority to realize stopping crime really does stop crime."

**The Pattern:** Take a position that goes against conventional wisdom, then show why it's actually the most reasonable stance.

**Application:** When writing, ask:
- What does everyone believe about this topic?
- What's the opposite position?
- Is the opposite position actually more obvious and reasonable?
- If so, make that your point

### 2. Calling Out the Obvious

**What it is:** Pointing out things everyone knows but no one says, especially about media, institutions, and groupthink.

**Examples:**
- "Of course, none of them read the 206-page report"
- "the press entered its standard defensive position for left-wing violence"
- "I mean, obviously, doing nothing tanks your brain activity (thank you, science)"

**The Pattern:** State what everyone is thinking but won't admit. Say it plainly. Add light sarcasm if appropriate.

**Application:** When writing, identify what your audience already knows but feels forbidden to say. Say it. They'll feel seen.

### 3. The "Actually, You're the Problem" Move

**What it is:** Flipping the script to show how the people complaining are often the ones causing the issue.

**Examples:**
- "O accursed parents of modernity, you've forgotten that you guys are literally adults"
- "if democracy is really under threat, it's not from [scapegoat]… it's from the man in the mirror"

**The Pattern:** When someone complains about Problem X, show how they're actually contributing to Problem X.

**Application:** Use this move when the conventional wisdom blames the wrong party.

### 4. The "This Is Ridiculous" Framework

**What it is:** Taking something that's being treated as serious and showing how absurd it actually is.

**Examples:**
- Treating a bike accident lawsuit like "the defining struggle of our time. A spiritual war between the forces of progress and innovation"
- Treating fare gate improvements like a breakthrough worthy of global attention

**The Pattern:** Take something overblown. Describe it with exaggerated gravity. Let the absurdity speak for itself.

### 5. The "Let's Be Honest" Approach

**What it is:** Cutting through the BS and saying what everyone's thinking but won't say.

**Examples:**
- "Sorry haters, the only problem I see here is my own slow start"
- "I'm glad America is paying our star nerds more than our star basketball players (finally)"

**The Pattern:** Admit to motivations, biases, or truths that sound cynical but are actually honest.

---

## Key Stylistic Elements

### 1. Conversational Authenticity

**What it is:** Writing that sounds like a real person talking, not a press release or academic paper.

**How to achieve:**
- Use contractions (I've, you're, don't, can't)
- Mix short, punchy sentences with longer ones
- Ask rhetorical questions
- Use colloquial language
- Admit uncertainty when honest
- Speak directly to the reader

**Application:** Read your draft aloud. If it doesn't sound like you talking to a friend, it's too formal.

**Examples:**
- "I think it's safe to say we've reached the 'irrational exuberance' stage of the AI hype cycle"
- "You don't have to pay for your kids to play outside. You can just force them to, for free."
- "I don't need to tell you where I stand on reinstating the f***ing Ministry of Truth, but I do have one question"

### 2. Parenthetical Commentary

**What it is:** Adding quick, witty observations in parentheses that feel like side conversations.

**How to use:**
- Interrupt main thought with a side comment
- Use ellipses or em dashes to set apart
- Keep it brief
- Add tone: sarcasm, humor, aside, clarification

**Examples:**
- "with, from best I can tell, no product or business"
- "the Nobel Peace Prize Committee has it on their radar… *checks notes* making their fare gates slightly taller"

**Application:** When you want to add personality without formally structuring it, use parenthetical asides.

### 3. The "lol" and Casual Interjections

**What it is:** Strategic use of casual language to break tension and show personality.

**Examples:**
- "and nobody died lol"
- "meaning they just copy/pasted, lol"
- "cue tiny violin"
- "O accursed parents of modernity, you've forgotten that you guys are literally adults"

**When to use:**
- To add levity to serious points
- To signal you're not taking yourself seriously
- To break tension in heavy content
- To add personality without becoming cartoonish

**Application:** When you've made a serious point, use "lol" or casual interjection to show you're not pretentious about it.

### 4. Rhetorical Questions and Direct Address

**What it is:** Speaking directly to the reader as if they're in the room, using questions to make them think.

**Examples:**
- "Why are institutionalists SO pathologically fixated on restricting the free flow of information??"
- "O accursed parents of modernity, you've forgotten that you guys are literally adults"
- "Why are some leaders so determined to control information flow?"

**How to use:**
- Address reader directly ("you")
- Ask questions with implied answers
- Use "why" questions to probe motivations
- Use dramatic direct address

**Application:** When you want readers to feel implicated or included, address them directly.

### 5. The "Two Things" Structure

**What it is:** Breaking down complex topics into digestible, numbered points with personality.

**Example:**
- "Two things: unstructured time is the fundamental substrate for creativity. And, uh, have you guys tried just… dealing with this?"

**How to use:**
- Start with "Two things:" or "A few points:"
- Each point gets its own thought
- Can nest multiple layers
- Adds rhythm and scannability

### 1. The Setup and Punch

**Pattern:** Start with something serious, end with something unexpected.

**Examples:**

**Pattern:** Acknowledge the obvious, then add insight.

**Example:**
- "Of course, none of them read the 206-page report, which reveals 18 college students had 20min to write essays based on (boring) SAT prompts using only ChatGPT."

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### 1. Overdoing the Casual Language
**Bad:** "lol this is so crazy omg what even is happening rn"
**Good:** "This is genuinely surprising. What's happening here?"

### 2. Forced Humor
**Bad:** "Insert joke here because we need to be funny"
**Good:** "The irony here is almost too perfect"

### 3. Losing the Point in Style
**Bad:** "So many parentheticals (and side comments) that you (the reader) lose track"
**Good:** "Too many distractions can make the main point disappear"

### 4. Inappropriate Sarcasm
**Bad:** Sarcasm about real tragedies
**Good:** "That's certainly one approach to the problem"

### 5. Mean-Spirited Attacks
**Bad:** Personal attacks on individuals
**Good:** Criticism of ideas, positions, or systemic problems

### 6. Losing the Obvious Truth
**Bad:** Getting so clever that the main point disappears
**Good:** The obvious truth should still be obvious after you're done

# Sticky Sentences - Deep Dive

*Creating Memorable Statements Through Literary Techniques*

## Why Sticky Sentences Matter

Sticky sentences are the ones readers remember. They're quotable. They're the lines that stick with people after they've forgotten the rest of the piece. They're also usually the MOST important sentences in your writing—the ones that carry your core idea.

Master these techniques to make your most important statements unforgettable.

---

## 1. Alliteration

**Definition:** Using words with the same sound (usually first letter) in succession.

### How It Works

The repetition of sounds creates a pleasing rhythm and makes the phrase easier to remember. Your brain likes patterns, so alliteration leverages that preference.

### Examples

- "Specificity is the secret" - @ItsKieranDrew
- "The best jobs are neither decreed nor degreed" - Naval Ravikant
- "Proper preparation prevents poor performance"
- "Fail fast, fix faster"
- "Creative, conversational, clear"

### When to Use

- When you have a key principle to establish
- When emphasizing an important point
- When you want readers to quote you
- When you need rhythm in a short statement

### How to Write It

1. **Start with your core idea** - "Teaching methods need to be both structured and flexible"
2. **Identify key words** - structure, flexible
3. **Find alliterative versions** - "structured, supple support" or "flexible, focused framework"
4. **Test for meaning** - Does it still mean what you intended?
5. **Say it aloud** - Does it have good rhythm?

### Practice Examples

Create alliterative versions of:
- "Learning happens through doing"
- "Students need both rules and freedom"
- "Ideas improve when challenged"

---

## 2. Symmetry

**Definition:** Creating balance with parallel phrases or structures.

### How It Works

Symmetry creates a sense of completeness and inevitability. When two sides balance perfectly, it feels true. Your brain recognizes the pattern and finds it satisfying.

### Examples

- "Read for awareness. Write for understanding."
- "It's not 10,000 hours. It's 10,000 iterations." - Naval Ravikant
- "In silence, we find our voice. In stillness, we find our strength."
- "Create to discover. Delete to refine."
- "Teachers inspire. Students transpire."

### When to Use

- Comparing or contrasting two ideas
- Establishing opposing principles
- Creating a memorable paradox
- Making a philosophical point

### Structure Template

**[Action A] [Outcome A]. [Action B] [Outcome B].**
OR
**Not [False idea]. [True idea].**
OR
**[Thing 1] [Verb]. [Thing 2] [Verb].**

### How to Write It

1. **Identify your two ideas** - What two things are you comparing?
2. **Find parallel structure** - Can you express them in the same grammatical form?
3. **Test the balance** - Does each side have similar weight?
4. **Check the meaning** - Are you actually saying something true?
5. **Say it aloud** - Does it feel balanced?

### Practice Examples

Create symmetrical versions of:
- "You need to understand both theory and practice"
- "The best leaders are confident but humble"
- "Innovation requires both tradition and rebellion"

## 3. Contrast

**Definition:** Juxtaposing opposing ideas to emphasize their difference.

### How It Works

Contrast makes ideas more vivid by putting opposites next to each other. Your brain can't help but notice the difference, which makes both sides more memorable.

### Examples

- "To be everywhere is to be nowhere." - Letters from a Stoic
- "Be clear, not clever. Concise, not complex." - @ItsKieranDrew
- "Simple rules. Complex applications."
- "Confident, not arrogant. Humble, not weak."
- "We teach students what to think. We should teach them how."

### When to Use

- Clarifying misconceptions
- Establishing important distinctions
- Making a philosophy memorable
- Teaching what something is NOT

### Structure Template

**[True statement]. Not [false statement].**
OR
**[Opposite 1], Not [Opposite 2].**
OR
**To [Action 1] is to [Result 1]. To [Action 2] is to [Result 2].**

### How to Write It

1. **State the true idea** - What do you actually believe?
2. **Find the opposite misconception** - What do people wrongly believe?
3. **Juxtapose them** - Put them right next to each other
4. **Keep both balanced** - Both sides should have similar weight
5. **Say it aloud** - Does the contrast pop?

### Practice Examples

Create contrasting versions of:
- "Schools should be rigorous"
- "Students need structure"
- "Feedback should be honest"

## 4. Rhyme

**Definition:** Using similar ending sounds to create harmony and memorability.

### How It Works

Rhyme is immediately recognizable to your brain and creates a song-like quality. This makes phrases incredibly sticky and memorable. People will remember a rhyming phrase much longer than a non-rhyming one.

### Examples

- "Tell a story or lose your glory"
- "Plan to succeed, just plant the seed"
- "Fail to prepare, prepare to fail"
- "Act with intent, know where time went"

### When to Use

- When you want maximum memorability
- In mottos or core principles
- In taglines or key phrases
- When you need something quotable

### Rhyme Types

**Perfect rhyme:** Identical end sounds
- "grow" / "flow"
- "create" / "state"
- "share" / "care"

**Slant rhyme:** Similar but not identical sounds
- "home" / "come"
- "teach" / "reach"
- "plan" / "land"

**Assonance:** Repeating vowel sounds
- "deep sleep"
- "pain and strain"
- "bold, cold, told"

### How to Write It

1. **Identify your core idea** - What's the most important point?
2. **Find a natural rhyming word** - What rhymes with your key concept?
3. **Build the phrase** - Create a complete thought that ends in rhyme
4. **Test for meaning** - Is it true? Does it make sense?
5. **Say it aloud** - Does it flow naturally?

### Practice Examples

Create rhyming versions of:
- "Learning requires practice"
- "Mistakes are part of growth"
- "Questions are more important than answers"

## 5. Rhythm

**Definition:** Creating a pleasing cadence through sentence structure and pacing.

### How It Works

Rhythm makes prose sing. It's the musical quality of language. When rhythm is good, readers want to keep reading. When it's bad, they want to stop. Rhythm creates momentum.

### Examples

- "When you can't wait to share it, they can't help but read it"
- "Make your sentences sing"
- "Move from left to right when creating. Move from right to left when refining."
- "Create, delete, then share your work"

### Elements of Rhythm

**Short sentences** - Create urgency and emphasis
"She left. He waited. Nothing changed."

**Long sentences** - Create flow and complexity
"Moving through the morning light, walking slowly, thinking about what comes next, wondering if the choice was right."

**Alternating length** - Creates interesting pattern
"Short. Then a longer sentence that builds momentum. Then short again."

**Parallel structures** - Repeating patterns that build
"Create to discover. Delete to refine. Share to connect."

### How to Write It

1. **Identify the emotional tone** - Urgent? Contemplative? Energetic?
2. **Plan sentence length** - Which sentences should be short? Long?
3. **Create variation** - Mix it up; don't use same length repeatedly
4. **Use parallel structures** - For building momentum
5. **Say it aloud** - Does it have good flow?

### Practice Examples

Try different rhythms for:
- "Learning is important"
- "Students need independence"
- "Teaching is complicated"

## Combining Techniques for Maximum Stickiness

The most memorable phrases combine multiple techniques:

### Example 1: Alliteration + Symmetry
"Anger prepares us to fight. Fear prepares us to flee."
- Alliteration: "prepares" appears in both
- Symmetry: Perfect parallel structure

### Example 2: Contrast + Rhythm
"To be everywhere is to be nowhere."
- Contrast: Opposite destinations
- Rhythm: Balanced through repetition

### Example 3: Alliteration + Rhyme
"Deep in your mind, design your find"
- Alliteration: d-sounds
- Rhyme: mind/find

### Example 4: Symmetry + Rhythm
"Create your voice. Find your choice. Make your mark."
- Symmetry: Parallel structure
- Rhythm: Building momentum
- Bonus: Internal rhyme (voice/choice)

## The Process for Strengthening Any Sentence

### Step 1: Identify Your Most Important Sentence
What's the core idea? The thing readers must remember?

### Step 2: Try Each Technique
- **Alliteration version:** Can you add repeated sounds?
- **Symmetry version:** Can you create parallel structure?
- **Contrast version:** Can you juxtapose opposites?
- **Rhyme version:** Can you add rhyming sounds?
- **Rhythm version:** Can you improve the cadence?

### Step 3: Evaluate Each Version
- Does it preserve the meaning?
- Is it natural and not forced?
- Is it more memorable than the original?
- Would readers quote it?

### Step 4: Choose the Strongest Version
- Pick the one that feels most true AND most memorable
- Test by saying it aloud
- Ask if it's something you'd actually say

### Step 5: Refine
- Smooth out any awkwardness
- Make sure it flows naturally
- Verify the meaning is crystal clear

## Before and After Examples

### Example 1: Basic Education Statement

**Before:** "It's important to teach students in multiple ways"

**Attempts:**
- Alliteration: "Teach through techniques, tenets, and truths"
- Symmetry: "Teach for understanding, not just information"
- Contrast: "Teach to develop, not just deliver"
- Rhythm: "Teach in many ways. Reach many minds."

**Winner:** "Teach in many ways. Reach many minds." (Alliteration + Symmetry + Rhythm)

### Example 2: Learning Statement

**Before:** "Making mistakes helps students learn"

**Attempts:**
- Alliteration: "Failures forge fundamental learning"
- Symmetry: "Mistakes make minds. Confusion creates clarity."
- Contrast: "Not perfection but persistence. Not answers but attempts."
- Rhyme: "Stumble and tumble toward the leap"

**Winner:** "Mistakes make minds." (Alliteration + Symmetry)

### Example 3: Teaching Philosophy

**Before:** "Good teachers understand their students"

**Attempts:**
- Alliteration: "Teachers truly teach through understanding"
- Symmetry: "Know your students. See your students. Teach your students."
- Contrast: "Not teaching students what they should learn. Teaching students how they learn."
- Rhythm: "Know them. See them. Teach them truly."

**Winner:** "Know them. See them. Teach them." (Symmetry + Rhythm + Alliteration)

## Key Takeaway

The most memorable writing combines:
- **Strong ideas** (substance)
- **Literary techniques** (style)
- **Natural flow** (authenticity)

Master these five techniques and you'll create sentences that readers remember, quote, and share. Your most important ideas will stick with them long after they've forgotten the rest of your writing.

Remember: The goal is not to be fancy. The goal is to be memorable while staying true to your meaning.
